One magnet, three coils

A small, strong magnet spins on the motor shaft. Three coils stand around it, a third of a turn (120°) apart. As the magnet turns, its field sweeps past each coil. The changing magnetic flux through a coil induces a voltage in it: Faraday's law of induction, ε = −N dΦ/dt.

The same voltage, a third of a turn apart

Every coil sees the same pulse of field, but each one a third of a turn after the one before. So the three traces have the same shape and are shifted by a third of a period: a phase difference of 120°. The phase diagram shows this as three arrows 120° apart. Move an arm and its arrow moves by the same angle.

This is how power stations generate electricity

A power-station generator works the same way, on a vastly larger scale. A spinning magnet (the rotor) with three sets of windings around it produces three-phase alternating current. India's grid runs at 50 Hz and the US grid at 60 Hz; the phases are coloured red, yellow and blue in India, the colours used for the coils here. This magnet turns only a few times a second, so the signals are millivolts instead of kilovolts. The physics is the same.

Why the three add up to zero

Three equal waves, 120° apart, cancel at every instant. That is why a balanced three-phase load needs no return current in the neutral wire, and why three-phase transmission is so economical. The dashed trace is the measured sum: on this kit only a few per cent of one coil. What is left comes from small differences between the coils and their placing, and from harmonics. Any third harmonic (a component at three times the rotation frequency) is the same in all three coils, because three times 120° is 360°, so it adds instead of cancelling. Power engineers watch for these "triplen" harmonics in neutral wires.

Reverse the motor

Run the motor the other way and the order in which the coils peak reverses: 1 → 2 → 3 becomes 1 → 3 → 2. Swapping any two wires of a three-phase supply does the same, which is how a three-phase motor is made to run backwards.

How the kit measures

A 32-bit converter (TI ADS1262) reads the coils with a noise of a few microvolts, against signals of tens of millivolts. Every reading carries a time stamp from the kit's crystal clock, in microseconds. The page fits each coil over whole turns, and shows how much the phase scatters from one second to the next. The kit is built from a microcontroller board, a converter board, a hobby motor and 3D-printed parts. It connects to any laptop with Chrome or Edge, with no software to install.

Getting started

  1. Flash EMF_Precision_USB_3Coil (firmware 5.0.0) to the Feather ESP32-S2. The single-coil firmware 4.0.0 also connects, but then only coil 1 is shown.
  2. Open this page in Google Chrome or Microsoft Edge on a desktop or laptop. Close the Arduino Serial Monitor first: only one program can hold the port.
  3. Press Connect kit and choose the Feather. Start the motor with Forward.

Wiring

Coil 1 (fixed arm, 0°) red lead to A0, coil 2 to A1, coil 3 to A2; all three black leads to ACOM. The firmware puts 2.5 V bias on ACOM. Every coil must face the magnet the same way and have its red lead on the same end of the winding. A coil wired the other way round reads 180° off (for example 300° instead of 120°), and the page says so; tick flip for it or swap its leads.

Never plug a power adapter into a coil socket. The coil cables use the same DC barrel plugs as power adapters, and 9–12 V on A0–A2 can damage the converter.

Readings

  • Phase behind coil 1: how far coils 2 and 3 lag coil 1, 0–360°, from a fit to each coil at the rotation frequency. The ± value is the scatter over the last 10 seconds.
  • Sum of the three: the size of the sum of the three fundamentals, as a percentage of one coil. The small text gives the whole measured sum, harmonics included.
  • Line voltages: differences between coils. For a balanced set they are √3 ≈ 1.732 times the coil voltages and 30° shifted.

If something looks wrong

  • Angle sweep with two arms: untick use for coil 3 (or whichever coil is off the bench). The strip then shows the angle between the two coils and their amplitude ratio.
  • Only coil 1 moves: the kit is running the single-coil firmware, or coils 2 and 3 are unplugged.
  • A coil leaks into the next one (a coil with its socket shorted still shows a signal): raise SCAN_DELAY_CODE or set DISCARD_AFTER_SWITCH = 1 in the firmware's Config.h.
  • Amplitudes differ a lot: the coils are at different distances from the magnet, or have different numbers of turns. See Coil balance.
  • Phases near 240° and 120° instead of 120° and 240°: the motor runs the other way. Nothing is wrong.
